My First 90 Days — A Working Thesis, Not a Claim About AMS

Four areas I'd assess and start building in, structured the way a cost-engineering function like this typically needs to be organized — and the operating rhythm I'd expect to work inside.

What this is — and isn't. This is my own hypothesis for how I'd approach this seat, built from general, well-established patterns in multi-site manufacturing cost engineering — not a claim about AMS's actual current state, systems, or org chart. I don't have that information, and I'm not implying otherwise. 1Cost Modeling, Quoting & Telemetry Automation

  • Quoting-to-actual drift. Static, spreadsheet-based quoting models diverging from real shop-floor costs once a part family moves past prototype volume — a near-universal pattern wherever quoting predates an automated actuals feedback loop.
  • Uniform overhead flattening work-center economics. Spreading facility overhead evenly across machines of very different capital intensity (a manual deburring station vs. a 5-axis mill) systematically misprices both.
  • Additive manufacturing's hidden cost tail. Costing DMLS/SLA jobs on laser runtime alone routinely misses powder-reclaim ratios, shielding-gas consumption, and post-processing — a known blind spot in additive costing generally.
Days 1-30Audit a sample of historical work orders against quoted cost to baseline the real drift, by part family and process.
Days 31-60Build the should-cost logic that closes the gap the audit found, starting with the highest-volume part family.
Days 61-90Pilot the automated actuals feedback loop on one part family before proposing a wider rollout.

2Operational Variance, Material Yield & Labor Productivity

  • Buy-to-fly waste in precision machining. Aerospace-grade billet milling routinely running 6:1+ material-to-finished-part ratios, priced into the model as sunk scrap rather than a tracked, reducible cost.
  • Routing standards that never get revisited. A cycle time set at first prototype release, then never re-timed — labor-efficiency variance drifts quietly for months before a monthly close surfaces it.
  • Scrap and rework cleared informally. Without a documented disposition trail, the true cost of non-quality never reaches the P&L in the first place.
Days 1-30Audit scrap/rework logging discipline and routing staleness — find out what's actually being captured today.
Days 31-60Build the variance-detection and disposition-tracking mechanism the audit shows is missing.
Days 61-90Automate the reporting last, once the underlying data capture is trustworthy enough to automate.

3Finance, P&L Ownership & Absorption Accounting

  • Unabsorbed capacity during ramp lulls. Idle machine time between prototype releases silently erodes the P&L as an unfavorable volume variance nobody flagged in advance.
  • Cost-avoidance claims that don't survive finance scrutiny. Benchmarked against stale or non-binding supplier quotes instead of a real, defensible market baseline.
  • Manual month-end variance reconciliation. Delays close and buries the real shop-floor driver under a spreadsheet instead of surfacing it.
Days 1-30Learn what absorption and cost-avoidance methodology finance will actually accept before proposing a new one.
Days 31-60Build the automated variance bridge against that accepted methodology.
Days 61-90Get it into the monthly close cadence as the standing source, not a one-off exercise.

4Strategic Capital, Equipment Scaling & Build-vs-Buy

  • Make-vs-buy calls driven by lead time, not cost. A real decision needs the marginal vs. fully-burdened cost comparison against the external quote, not just "who can deliver fastest."
  • Tooling expensed against a single job. Specialized fixtures and build plates expensed against one prototype run instead of amortized across the production runs that actually use them.
  • CapEx requests without a payback model. Major equipment asks (multi-laser additive, 5-axis mill-turn) create friction at planning review when there's no NPV/payback behind them.
Days 1-30Build the build-vs-buy and payback-model logic as a reusable tool, not a one-time analysis.
Days 31-60Validate it against a couple of real historical decisions to see if it would have changed the call.
Days 61-90Formalize it into the standard capital-request process ahead of the next planning cycle.
